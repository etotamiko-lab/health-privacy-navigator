The core idea

The protection does not follow the data

The same lab result can be fully protected, partly protected, or unprotected depending on where it sits.

In the hospital EHRHIPAA applies. Part 2 too if it is SUD treatment data.
Patient portal or APIHIPAA right of access and Cures Act rules push the data to the patient.
On the patient's phoneHIPAA no longer applies. The patient controls it.
Uploaded to an AI chatbotThe company's privacy policy, the FTC, and state law, if any. Training on chats depends on the terms.
Coverage

HIPAA covers who, not what

HIPAA came from a 1996 law about insurance portability and electronic claims. It reaches health plans, clearinghouses, and providers that bill electronically, plus their business associates. Wearables, symptom searches, location history near a clinic, consumer apps, and many cash-pay telehealth services fall outside it.

Some states close part of this gap. Washington's My Health My Data Act, Nevada, and Connecticut regulate consumer health data. Texas and California apply their medical privacy laws to entities HIPAA misses.

De-identification

Lower risk is not no risk

HIPAA de-identified data is outside HIPAA. The Safe Harbor list of 18 identifiers dates from 2000. Expert Determination considers what else a recipient could link to. When a recipient re-identifies data, HHS has no authority to act, because the data was never PHI in their hands.

AI models make linkage easier, and large language models can memorize training data. State definitions of de-identified data differ from HIPAA's.

Consent

Opt-in, opt-out, and the right to limit

Most state consumer privacy laws treat health data as sensitive and require opt-in consent before a business processes it. California gives a right to limit use instead; Utah and Iowa use notice and opt-out. Maryland bans selling sensitive data outright. Most of these laws exempt HIPAA covered entities or PHI, and many exempt nonprofits and smaller businesses.

Ghost AI

Unapproved tools move PHI out of HIPAA

When staff paste patient information into a consumer AI tool with no business associate agreement, the data leaves the controls HIPAA requires. That is a potential breach for the covered entity, and the AI company has no HIPAA duties toward it. A 2026 survey found about 1 in 5 health workers admitted using unapproved AI tools.

    Research

    Where IRBs fit

    Many state laws exempt research conducted under the Common Rule or FDA rules, or clinical trial data collected under them. That assumes those rules protect privacy and AI decisions, which is often not true for AI studies. Non-federally funded studies where an institution has unchecked the box may fall into the gap. Colorado's new law excludes IND drug trials but does not name IDE device studies.

    For patients and care partners

    Questions to ask when AI touches your care

    1. What is the AI doing in my care, and who reviews its output?
    2. What information about me is collected, shared, or kept, and for how long?
    3. Is the AI scribe recording part of my record, and can I get a copy?
    4. Are risk scores or predictions about me used, and can I see them?
    5. Can an AI output affect my treatment, prior authorization, or coverage?
    6. What happens if I say no or change my mind? What are the alternatives?
    7. Can I opt out, or ask for my data to be deleted?
    8. Will my conversations be used to train the AI?
    9. Was this tool tested on people like me, with my condition, in my community?
    10. How do I correct wrong information or ask for a human review?
    11. If something goes wrong, who do I contact?
    12. How does the AI affect what I pay?
